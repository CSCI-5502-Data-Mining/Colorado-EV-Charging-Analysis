Colorado EV Charging Access & Adoption

Investigating where Colorado’s public EV charging network aligns with current demand — and where gaps may remain for lower-income, renter-heavy, and rural communities.

View Research Questions See Project Plan

A. Introduction

Why this problem matters

Colorado is expanding electric-vehicle infrastructure, but charger availability may not match current EV ownership, expected demand, or community need. Residents who rent, live in multifamily housing, or live in lower-density rural areas often have fewer opportunities to charge at home and may depend more on public infrastructure.

The decision this project supports is practical: where additional public charging would better balance demand and equity, rather than only following where EVs already cluster. A demand-only view could favor affluent neighborhoods that already have higher EV ownership.

Official station-level map (not our figure): Colorado Information Marketplace charging map.

Demand

Where does EV ownership suggest a need for more public charging capacity?

Equity

Which communities may face greater barriers to home charging or public charging access?

Geography

How do patterns differ between urban, suburban, and rural parts of Colorado?

Key stakeholders

  • Current and prospective EV owners, especially those without reliable home charging
  • Lower-income and renter-heavy communities
  • Rural communities and small municipalities
  • Colorado Energy Office, CDOT, and local transportation or planning agencies
  • Utilities, charging networks, and infrastructure planners

What is already known, and the gap

National and state work already tracks EV registrations, public charger locations, and corridor charging plans. Research also shows that charging availability and EV adoption often vary with income, housing type, and urban/rural setting. Colorado planning documents treat rural and disproportionately impacted communities as a priority for charging investment.

The unanswered question for this project is more local: which Colorado areas combine expected charging demand with limited existing access, once socioeconomic and housing conditions are taken into account? Statewide totals and corridor maps do not by themselves show ZIP- or community-level mismatches between registrations, ports, and who can charge at home.

Semester blueprint

What the team plans to investigate this semester (framing first; modeling later):

  1. Acquire and document Colorado EV registration, charging, usage, and community datasets.
  2. Standardize records to a common geographic level and record join limitations (ZIP vs. ZCTA, snapshot dates).
  3. Describe how EV adoption relates to income, housing, and urban/rural setting, and how charger availability compares.
  4. Identify areas that appear underserved relative to current or expected demand, with an equity-aware priority view.
  5. Communicate results with maps and a transparent scoring framework — not a black-box ranking.

B. Research Questions

Questions guiding the investigation

These refine the approved Milestone 0 question: What factors are associated with EV adoption in Colorado, and which areas appear underserved by the existing charging network? They are written to be answerable with the planned data. Model names are intentionally omitted.

RQ1

Relationship / pattern

How do income and housing characteristics (including affordability and renter share, as data allow) relate to EV adoption and public charger availability across Colorado?

RQ2

Comparative

Which Colorado areas have high EV adoption relative to the number of available public charging ports?

RQ3

Comparative

How does charging-port density differ between urban and rural areas of Colorado?

RQ4

Descriptive / identification

Which Colorado areas show the greatest combination of expected charging demand and limited existing charging access?

RQ1–RQ3 map directly to the Milestone 0 sub-questions. RQ4 is the intended map/priority-score output. We are not forcing a predictive modeling question at this stage.

Background / Related Work

What prior work tells us

Each source below is summarized for what it contributes to our framing, not listed as a citation-only bullet.

Charging infrastructure, income, and adoption

Gifford and Barbier (2026) study county-level charger expansion in Washington State (2019–2023). They find that new public chargers are associated with higher battery-EV adoption, but the effect is concentrated in high-income counties, and fast chargers show larger effects than slower chargers. Plug-in hybrids do not show the same pattern. For our project, this supports treating income as a conditioning factor, not assuming that “more chargers everywhere” has a uniform adoption effect, and being cautious about demand-only investment rules that could widen gaps.

Gifford & Barbier, 2026, Energy Policy.

DOI 10.1016/j.enpol.2026.115098 ↗

National inequality in EV ownership and charging

Lee et al. (2025) quantify income and racial/ethnic inequality in U.S. plug-in EV adoption and public charging relative to conventional vehicles and gas stations. They report that charging-infrastructure income inequality is about three times larger than that of gas stations, with large variation across states and metro areas, and that adoption inequality and infrastructure inequality do not always move together. This motivates a Colorado-specific look: national averages can hide local mismatches, which is exactly the gap our ZIP/community analysis is meant to address.

Lee et al., 2025, Applied Energy.

DOI 10.1016/j.apenergy.2024.124795 ↗

Colorado charging policy context

Colorado’s 2022 NEVI Plan (CDOT, with Colorado Energy Office partnership described in the plan) frames public charging as necessary for statewide EV targets and explicitly calls out remaining gaps in rural and disproportionately impacted communities, as well as urban challenges such as apartment and condo residents without affordable home charging. It is a planning document, not a ZIP-level equity analysis. It gives us the decision context and stakeholder language; it does not replace our empirical comparison of registrations, ports, and community characteristics.

Colorado Department of Transportation, 2022.

Colorado NEVI Plan (PDF) ↗

D. Proposal Overview

Approved idea, data, and constraints

Problem

Colorado’s EV charging infrastructure may not fully reflect current demand or the needs of underserved communities. Lower-income and renter-heavy areas may have less access to home charging and fewer public options.

Goal

Identify socioeconomic and geographic factors associated with EV adoption in Colorado and determine where additional public charging could best balance demand and equity.

Intended output (Milestone 0): a Colorado map or priority score identifying areas with high expected charging demand and limited existing access.

Change after Milestone 0

The core problem, goal, and charging/EV sources are unchanged. We added U.S. Census ACS community estimates to the planned stack because the approved questions require income and housing context, which were not in the Milestone 0 data table. Exact ACS tables, years, and the ZIP/ZCTA join still need to be specified in a later milestone. Research questions were restated more tightly (four questions instead of one umbrella question plus two bullets) so each can be mapped to evidence.

Major constraints (from Milestone 0)

Equity bias

Existing EV ownership may already reflect income inequality. Mitigation: include income, renter share, and charger access in an equity-aware priority view — not demand alone.

Demand proxy

Registration location is not where a vehicle is charged. Treat registrations as residential demand estimates and combine them with charger and population data.

Usage coverage

EValuateCO covers only selected funded stations and may not represent statewide behavior.

Temporal mismatch

Datasets may represent different years or update schedules. Snapshot dates will be recorded during data prep.
